Somebody sent me a link to VoteHub this week, a site that tracks polling averages, and the first thing I noticed wasn't a poll. It was Kalshi, everywhere, the way a sportsbook logo is everywhere on a football broadcast. Two years ago it was illegal to bet on an American election in this country. Now the betting companies run ads, sponsor coverage, and in at least one case settle their bets on a polling site's numbers. I think that taints the whole thing, and I don't think it's an accident that it happened this fast.

## From Banned to Sponsored in Two Years

Kalshi sued the CFTC for the right to list election contracts and won in federal court in September 2024. The CFTC appealed, but then the administration changed, and in May 2025 the agency moved to drop its own appeal. Polymarket, which the CFTC fined in 2022 and ordered to keep Americans off its platform, bought a licensed US exchange in 2025 and got the CFTC to sign off on its return.

What followed was the normalization. The Associated Press licensed its election data to Kalshi in March 2026, and Fox signed a deal to work Kalshi odds into its coverage. Kalshi launched a "Midterms Hub" that puts its odds right next to the polls, as if the two were the same kind of information.

They aren't. A poll asks people what they think. A market asks people with money what they're willing to bet, and those are two different groups of people.

## Who Is Actually Placing the Bets

The companies like to call this "the wisdom of the crowd." I'd want to know who's in the crowd before I took its wisdom seriously.

Nobody can give you clean demographics, since Polymarket runs on crypto wallets and most accounts are anonymous. What we do know is where the money ends up, and it ends up in a handful of wallets. A Decrypt analysis after the 2024 election found that 86% of Polymarket traders lost money. The winnings went to a small group of large accounts, and one French trader, known as Théo, spread his bets across four anonymous accounts and cleared about $79 million on Trump winning. He says he had no political agenda. Maybe so. But when one person can move the headline odds on a presidential race, "the crowd" is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

So the odds aren't a picture of what Americans think. They're a picture of what a small, rich, self-selected group of gamblers thinks, weighted by how much each one is willing to put down. Then that number gets shown on the news next to a poll of actual voters.

## A Regulator With a Stake

Here is the part I can't get past. These platforms exist in the United States because one administration's regulators decided to let them. And the President's son gets paid by both of them.

Donald Trump Jr. joined Kalshi as a strategic advisor in January 2025, with equity worth more than $300,000 at the time. In August 2025, 1789 Capital, the venture firm where he's a partner, invested in Polymarket, and he joined its advisory board too. The CFTC that dropped the Kalshi appeal is now run by a Trump appointee who has backed the platforms against states trying to rein them in.

Think about what that means for the companies' incentives. Their whole legal footing depends on the CFTC staying friendly, and the CFTC stays friendly for exactly as long as the current party holds power. I'm not claiming anyone rigs a market. I'm saying a business whose survival depends on one political outcome has a reason to prefer that outcome, and these businesses now sit right next to the news, telling you who's going to win.

## When the Pollster Settles the Bet

VoteHub is where this gets strange. Kalshi uses VoteHub's polling averages on its Midterms Hub. It goes further than that, though. Kalshi's public contract terms for some of its markets name VoteHub's "time-adjusted average" as the number that decides whether a bet pays out.

I'm not accusing VoteHub of anything. It may run a perfectly honest operation. But a polling average used to be something you read, and now it's something people have money riding on. Every choice behind that average, like which polls get included, how much each one counts, and when it updates, now moves money from one bettor to another. That's a new kind of pressure on a part of our elections whose only job was to measure things, and nobody voted to put it there.

## The Odds Aren't Neutral

The industry's defense is that markets just predict and don't change anything. I don't buy it.

People treat odds on the evening news as a forecast. A candidate who looks like a long shot has a harder time raising money, getting coverage, and convincing their own voters that showing up matters. Those odds come from a thin, wealthy pool of traders, and one whale with a hunch can move them. Election coverage has always had a horse-race problem. Now the horse race has a sportsbook attached to it, and the sportsbook has the President's son on the payroll.

## Putting It Together

Election betting went from illegal to sponsored content in about two years, helped along by a CFTC that stopped fighting it and an administration whose family profits from it. The "crowd" setting the odds is a small, rich, unrepresentative group, and in at least one case, the bets settle on a polling site's own numbers. I think Congress should ban election contracts outright, and polling and election data should stay out of the betting business. Democracy shouldn't come with a spread.
